# Chapter 4. The Seifert–Van Kampen theorem

Source: handwritten lecture notes of 2026-10-15 (Massey, Chapter IV).

!!! note "Notation"
    As before, $\pi(X)$ is the fundamental group with a base point fixed once and for
    all, every unlabelled map between fundamental groups is induced by an inclusion,
    $\simeq$ is used only for homotopic maps, and $G_1 * G_2$ is the free product
    (§3.7).

## 4.1 Motivation

**Question.** How do we compute $\pi(T^2)$, $\pi(\mathbb{RP}^2)$,
$\pi(T^2\#T^2)$, etc.?

**Observation.** $T^2$ and $\mathbb{RP}^2$ can be decomposed into two "relatively
simple" parts. Let $D$ be a small (open) disc in the interior of the square.

| | the square | ① remove a small disc | ② remove the boundary of the square |
|---|---|---|---|
| $T^2$ | $aba^{-1}b^{-1}$ | $T^2\setminus D$ has a deformation retract in the shape "$\infty$" ($a\vee b$) | $T^2\setminus\partial(\text{square})\cong\mathbb{R}^2$ |
| $\mathbb{RP}^2$ | $abab$ | $\mathbb{RP}^2\setminus D\cong$ Möbius band, which has its center circle as a deformation retract | $\mathbb{RP}^2\setminus\partial(\text{square})\cong\mathbb{R}^2$ |

**Goal.** Write $X=U\cup V$ such that $U$, $V$ and $U\cap V$ are "sufficiently"
simple, and then compute $\pi(X)$ from $\pi(U)$, $\pi(V)$ (and $\pi(U\cap V)$).

## 4.2 Statement of the theorem

**Assumptions.** $U,V$ are open, $X=U\cup V$, and $U$, $V$, $U\cap V$ are pathwise
connected. Choose the base point $x_0\in U\cap V$ for all fundamental groups.

Let

$$
\begin{array}{ccccc}
\pi(U\cap V) & \xrightarrow{\ \varphi_1\ } & \pi(U) & \xrightarrow{\ \psi_1\ } & \pi(X),\\[2pt]
\pi(U\cap V) & \xrightarrow{\ \varphi_2\ } & \pi(V) & \xrightarrow{\ \psi_2\ } & \pi(X),\\[2pt]
\pi(U\cap V) & & \xrightarrow{\qquad\ \psi_3\qquad\ } & & \pi(X),
\end{array}
$$

where $\varphi_i,\psi_i$ are the homomorphisms induced by the inclusions
$U\cap V\hookrightarrow U\hookrightarrow X$, etc. Note
$\psi_3 = \psi_1\varphi_1 = \psi_2\varphi_2$.

> **Theorem 1 (Seifert–Van Kampen).** Given any group $H$ and homomorphisms
> $\rho_1:\pi(U)\to H$, $\rho_2:\pi(V)\to H$, $\rho_3:\pi(U\cap V)\to H$ such that
> $\rho_1\varphi_1=\rho_3=\rho_2\varphi_2$, there exists a **unique** homomorphism
> $\sigma:\pi(X)\to H$ such that
>
> $$
> \sigma\circ\psi_1=\rho_1,\qquad \sigma\circ\psi_2=\rho_2,\qquad \sigma\circ\psi_3=\rho_3 .
> $$

In words: $\pi(X)$ is universal among groups receiving compatible homomorphisms from
$\pi(U)$ and $\pi(V)$ — it is the free product $\pi(U)*\pi(V)$ with the images of
$\pi(U\cap V)$ identified.

## 4.3 Generalization

**Assumptions.**

1. $\{U_\lambda\}_{\lambda\in\Lambda}$ is a collection of open sets of $X$;
2. $\{U_\lambda\}_{\lambda\in\Lambda}$ is closed under (finite) intersection, i.e.
   $U_{\lambda_1}\cap U_{\lambda_2}=U_\lambda$ for some $\lambda\in\Lambda$;
3. $X=\bigcup_{\lambda\in\Lambda}U_\lambda$, with every $U_\lambda$ pathwise connected.

Choose a base point $x_0\in U_\lambda$ for all $\lambda\in\Lambda$. For
$U_\lambda\subseteq U_\mu$ let

$$
\pi(U_\lambda)\xrightarrow{\ \varphi_{\lambda\mu}\ }\pi(U_\mu)\xrightarrow{\ \psi_\mu\ }\pi(X)
$$

be the homomorphisms induced by inclusions.

> **Theorem 1′.** Given any group $H$ and homomorphisms
> $\{\rho_\lambda:\pi(U_\lambda)\to H\}_{\lambda\in\Lambda}$ such that
> $\rho_\mu\circ\varphi_{\lambda\mu}=\rho_\lambda$ for all $\lambda,\mu$ with
> $U_\lambda\subseteq U_\mu$, there exists a unique homomorphism
> $\sigma:\pi(X)\to H$ such that $\sigma\circ\psi_\lambda=\rho_\lambda$ for all
> $\lambda$.

Theorem 1 is the case $\{U_\lambda\}=\{U,V,U\cap V\}$.

## 4.4 Key idea of the proof

Uniqueness follows from the lemma below: a homomorphism is determined by its values
on a generating set, and $\sigma$ is prescribed on each
$\psi_\lambda(\pi(U_\lambda))$.

> **Lemma.** $\pi(X)$ is generated by
> $\{\psi_\lambda(\pi(U_\lambda))\}_{\lambda\in\Lambda}$.

*Proof.* Given $\alpha\in\pi(X)$, take a loop $f:I\to X$ with $[f]=\alpha$. Since
$I$ is a compact interval, $\{f^{-1}(U_\lambda)\}_{\lambda\in\Lambda}$ is an open
cover of a compact metric space; choose $n$ so large that $\frac1n$ is less than its
Lebesgue number. Consider the partition
$\{0,\frac1n,\frac2n,\dots,\frac{n-1}{n},1\}$ of $I$ and write

$$
J_i=\Big[\frac in,\frac{i+1}n\Big],\qquad f(J_i)\subseteq U_{\lambda_i}
\ \text{ for some }\lambda_i\in\Lambda,\quad 0\le i\le n-1 .
$$

For $1\le i\le n-1$ choose a path $g_i$ in $U_{\lambda_{i-1}}\cap U_{\lambda_i}$ from
$x_0$ to $f(\tfrac in)$; this is possible because
$U_{\lambda_{i-1}}\cap U_{\lambda_i}$ is again some $U_\lambda$ (assumption 2), so it
is pathwise connected and contains $x_0$.

Let $h_i:I\to J_i$ be the orientation-preserving linear homeomorphism and
$f_i=f|_{J_i}\circ h_i$:

$$
I\xrightarrow{\ h_i\ }J_i\xrightarrow{\ f|_{J_i}\ }X,\qquad f_i=f|_{J_i}\circ h_i .
$$

Then

$$
f_0\cdot g_1^{-1},\quad g_1\cdot f_1\cdot g_2^{-1},\quad g_2\cdot f_2\cdot g_3^{-1},\quad\dots,\quad
g_{n-2}\cdot f_{n-2}\cdot g_{n-1}^{-1},\quad g_{n-1}\cdot f_{n-1}
$$

are loops at $x_0$, the $i$-th one contained in $U_{\lambda_i}$. Thus

$$
\begin{aligned}
\alpha_0&=[f_0\cdot g_1^{-1}]\in\psi_{\lambda_0}\big(\pi(U_{\lambda_0})\big),\\
\alpha_1&=[g_1\cdot f_1\cdot g_2^{-1}]\in\psi_{\lambda_1}\big(\pi(U_{\lambda_1})\big),\\
&\ \ \vdots\\
\alpha_{n-2}&=[g_{n-2}\cdot f_{n-2}\cdot g_{n-1}^{-1}]\in\psi_{\lambda_{n-2}}\big(\pi(U_{\lambda_{n-2}})\big),\\
\alpha_{n-1}&=[g_{n-1}\cdot f_{n-1}]\in\psi_{\lambda_{n-1}}\big(\pi(U_{\lambda_{n-1}})\big),
\end{aligned}
$$

and, since each $g_i^{-1}\cdot g_i$ is equivalent to a constant path,

$$
\alpha=\alpha_0\cdot\alpha_1\cdots\alpha_{n-2}\cdot\alpha_{n-1}. \qquad\blacksquare
$$

!!! note "Why the partition is finite"
    The Lebesgue number $\delta$ depends only on the open cover
    $\{f^{-1}(U_\lambda)\}$, so one fixed $n>1/\delta$ works for the whole of $I$.
    Even if $f$ is a space-filling curve, every piece $f(J_i)$ lies in a single
    $U_{\lambda_i}$; we never cut at the times where $f$ crosses
    $\partial U_\lambda$, which could indeed happen infinitely often.

The **existence** part follows by showing that

$$
\sigma(\alpha)=\rho_{\lambda_0}(\alpha_0)\,\rho_{\lambda_1}(\alpha_1)\cdots\rho_{\lambda_{n-1}}(\alpha_{n-1})
$$

is independent of the choice of the decomposition
$\alpha=\alpha_0\cdots\alpha_{n-1}$ (skipped in class; §2.4 in the book).

## 4.5 Applications: $U\cap V$ simply connected

> **Theorem 2.** Follow the assumptions and notation of Theorem 1. If $U\cap V$ is
> simply connected, then
>
> $$
> \pi(X)\cong\pi(U)*\pi(V)
> $$
>
> with respect to $\{\psi_1,\psi_2\}$.

*Proof.* $\pi(U\cap V)=\{1\}$, so $\varphi_1,\varphi_2,\psi_3$ and $\rho_3$ are
trivial ($=0$).

- For every group $H$, the condition $\rho_1\varphi_1=\rho_3=\rho_2\varphi_2$ of
  Theorem 1 holds for **arbitrary** $\rho_1,\rho_2$.
- The condition $\sigma\psi_3=\rho_3$ holds for **arbitrary** $\sigma$.

So Theorem 1 says: for every $H$ and every pair $\rho_1,\rho_2$ there is a unique
$\sigma$ with $\sigma\psi_1=\rho_1$, $\sigma\psi_2=\rho_2$. This is exactly the
definition of the free product (§3.7), hence $\pi(X)\cong\pi(U)*\pi(V)$.
$\blacksquare$

**Example 1 (figure eight).** Let $X$ be the figure eight, $U$ an open set
consisting of the left loop together with a small open cross around the wedge point,
and $V$ the same for the right loop. Each of $U$, $V$ deformation retracts onto a
circle, so $\pi(U)\cong\mathbb{Z}$ and $\pi(V)\cong\mathbb{Z}$. Also
$U\cap V$ is the small cross, which is contractible, so $\pi(U\cap V)=\{1\}$. By the
Seifert–Van Kampen theorem,

$$
\pi(X)\cong\pi(U)*\pi(V)\cong\mathbb{Z}*\mathbb{Z}.
$$

**Example 2 (more generally).** A bouquet of three circles: take $U$ = one loop plus
a neighbourhood of the wedge point and $V$ = the other two loops plus that
neighbourhood. Then $\pi(U\cap V)=\{1\}$ and

$$
\pi(X)\cong\pi(U)*\pi(V)\cong\mathbb{Z}*\mathbb{Z}*\mathbb{Z}.
$$

By induction, for a bouquet of $n$ circles ("$n$ lobes"),
$\pi(X)\cong\prod^{*}_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{Z}$.

**Example 3.** Let $X=S^2\setminus\{n\text{ points}\}$. Removing one point gives
$\mathbb{R}^2$ (stereographic projection), so

- $n=1$: $\pi(X)\cong\pi(\mathbb{R}^2)\cong\{1\}$;
- $n=2$: $\pi(X)\cong\pi(\mathbb{R}^2\setminus\{1\text{ pt}\})\cong\pi(S^1)\cong\mathbb{Z}$;
- $n=3$: $\pi(X)\cong\pi(\mathbb{R}^2\setminus\{2\text{ pts}\})\cong\mathbb{Z}*\mathbb{Z}$,
  since $\mathbb{R}^2\setminus\{2\text{ pts}\}$ deformation retracts onto a figure
  eight;
- $n=4$: $\pi(X)\cong\mathbb{Z}*\mathbb{Z}*\mathbb{Z}$.

More generally,

$$
\pi\big(S^2\setminus\{n\text{ points}\}\big)\cong\pi\big(\mathbb{R}^2\setminus\{n-1\text{ points}\}\big)\cong\prod^{*}_{i=1}^{n-1}\mathbb{Z}.
$$

## 4.6 Applications: $V$ simply connected

> **Theorem 3.** Follow the assumptions and notation of Theorem 1. If $V$ is simply
> connected, then
>
> $$
> \pi(X)\cong\pi(U)/\ker\psi_1=\pi(U)/N,
> $$
>
> where $N$ is the smallest normal subgroup of $\pi(U)$ containing
> $\operatorname{im}\varphi_1=\varphi_1\big(\pi(U\cap V)\big)$.

*Proof.* The diagram

$$
\begin{array}{ccccc}
 & & \pi(U) & & \\
 & \nearrow^{\varphi_1} & & \searrow^{\psi_1} & \\
\pi(U\cap V) & & \xrightarrow{\ \psi_3\ } & & \pi(X)\\
 & \searrow_{\varphi_2} & & \nearrow_{\psi_2} & \\
 & & \pi(V)=\{1\} & &
\end{array}
$$

commutes. Since $\pi(V)=\{1\}$, $\psi_2=0$, hence $\psi_3=\psi_2\varphi_2=0$, hence
$\psi_1\varphi_1=\psi_3=0$, i.e. $\operatorname{im}\varphi_1\subseteq\ker\psi_1$.

- By the Lemma, $\pi(X)$ is generated by $\psi_1(\pi(U))$ and
  $\psi_2(\pi(V))=\{1\}$, so $\psi_1$ is **surjective**. Therefore
  $\pi(X)\cong\pi(U)/\ker\psi_1$.
- On the other hand, $\ker\psi_1$ is a normal subgroup containing
  $\operatorname{im}\varphi_1$, so $N\subseteq\ker\psi_1$.

For the reverse inclusion let $H:=\pi(U)/N$, let $\rho_1:\pi(U)\to H$ be the
projection, and $\rho_2=0$, $\rho_3=0$. Then
$\rho_1\varphi_1=0=\rho_3=\rho_2\varphi_2$ because $\operatorname{im}\varphi_1\subseteq N$.
By the Seifert–Van Kampen theorem there is a unique homomorphism
$\sigma:\pi(X)\to H$ with $\sigma\psi_1=\rho_1$ (and $\sigma\psi_2=0$,
$\sigma\psi_3=0$). Consequently

$$
\ker\psi_1\subseteq\ker(\sigma\circ\psi_1)=\ker\rho_1=N,
$$

and therefore $\ker\psi_1=N$. $\blacksquare$

**Example 4 (the torus).** We already know
$\pi(T^2)\cong\pi(S^1\times S^1)\cong\pi(S^1)\times\pi(S^1)\cong\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}$.
Let us recover this from Theorem 3.

Express $T^2$ as the square with $aba^{-1}b^{-1}$ and let $c$ be the center point of
the square. Put

$$
U=T^2\setminus\{c\},\qquad V=\{\text{interior of the square}\},\qquad
U\cap V=\{\text{interior of the square}\}\setminus\{c\}.
$$

- $\pi(U\cap V)\cong\mathbb{Z}$, the free group with generator $\gamma$, a small
  loop around $c$.
- $\pi(U)\cong\pi(\infty)\cong\mathbb{Z}*\mathbb{Z}=:F(a,b)$, the free group
  generated by $a,b$, since $U$ has the "$\infty$"-shaped curve $a\vee b$ (the image of
  $\partial(\text{square})$) as a deformation retract.
- $V$ is an open disc, so it is simply connected.
- Under $\varphi_1:\pi(U\cap V)\to\pi(U)$, pushing $\gamma$ out to the boundary of
  the square gives $\varphi_1(\gamma)=aba^{-1}b^{-1}$.

<video class="anim" src="assets/animations/PuncturedTorusRetract.mp4" controls loop muted autoplay playsinline></video>

By Theorem 3,

$$
\pi(T^2)\cong\pi(U)/N
=F(a,b)\big/\big[F(a,b),F(a,b)\big]
=\text{the free abelian group with generators }\{a,b\}
\cong\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z},
$$

where $N$ is the smallest normal subgroup containing $\{aba^{-1}b^{-1}\}$, which is
the commutator subgroup of $F(a,b)$ (§3.8).

!!! note "Base points"
    $\gamma$ is based at a point of $U\cap V$, while the loops $a,b$ live on the
    boundary of the square. To compare them, join the two base points by a path in
    $U$; this changes $\varphi_1(\gamma)$ only by conjugation, which does not change
    the normal subgroup $N$.
