# Chapter 6. Introduction to homology theory

Source: lecture notes provided by the instructor (weeks of 2026-11-12 and 2026-11-19;
Massey, Chapters VI–VII).

!!! note "What homology is for (Massey §VI.2)"
    Homology assigns to every space $X$ abelian groups $H_0(X),H_1(X),H_2(X),\dots$
    and to every map $f:X\to Y$ homomorphisms $f_*:H_n(X)\to H_n(Y)$, such that
    homotopic maps induce the same homomorphism, homotopy equivalent spaces have
    isomorphic groups, $H_0(X)$ is free abelian of rank $=\#$ path components, and,
    for $X$ pathwise connected, $H_1(X)\cong\pi(X)/[\pi(X),\pi(X)]$. The groups
    $H_n$ for $n\ge2$ detect "higher-dimensional holes" that $\pi(X)$ cannot see.

## 6.1 Singular cubes and chains

> **Definition.** A *singular $n$-cube* in a topological space $X$ is a continuous
> map $T:I^n\to X$. We say $T$ is *degenerate* if $T(x_1,\dots,x_n)$ is independent
> of $x_i$ for some $i$.

Let

- $Q_n(X)$ = the free abelian group generated by the set of singular $n$-cubes
  (elements of $Q_n(X)$: $\sum_{i=1}^{N}n_iT_i$, $n_i\in\mathbb{Z}$, $T_i$ singular
  $n$-cubes);
- $D_n(X)$ = the subgroup of $Q_n(X)$ generated by degenerate $n$-cubes;
- $C_n(X)=Q_n(X)/D_n(X)$, $n\ge0$: the *(cubical singular) $n$-chains*. This is the
  free abelian group generated by the non-degenerate $n$-cubes.

We may set $C_n(X)=\{0\}$ for $n<0$.

**Face operators.** For a singular $n$-cube $T$ and $1\le i\le n$, define
$A_iT,B_iT:I^{n-1}\to X$ by

$$
\begin{aligned}
A_iT(x_1,\dots,x_{n-1})&=T(x_1,\dots,x_{i-1},0,x_i,\dots,x_{n-1}),\\
B_iT(x_1,\dots,x_{n-1})&=T(x_1,\dots,x_{i-1},1,x_i,\dots,x_{n-1}).
\end{aligned}
$$

**Observe.** For $i<j$,

$$
A_iA_j(T)=A_{j-1}A_i(T),\qquad B_iB_j(T)=B_{j-1}B_i(T),\qquad
A_iB_j(T)=B_{j-1}A_i(T),\qquad B_iA_j(T)=A_{j-1}B_i(T).
\tag{$\ast$}
$$

## 6.2 The boundary operator and homology groups

**Boundary operators.**

$$
\partial_nT=\sum_{i=1}^n(-1)^i\big(A_iT-B_iT\big).
$$

For example, for $n=2$:

$$
\partial_2T=-A_1T+A_2T+B_1T-B_2T\qquad(\text{the oriented boundary of }T).
$$

Extend $\partial_n$ to a homomorphism $\partial_n:Q_n(X)\to Q_{n-1}(X)$.

<video class="anim" src="assets/animations/CubeBoundary.mp4" controls loop muted autoplay playsinline></video>

> **Lemma 1.** $\partial_{n-1}\circ\partial_n:Q_n(X)\to Q_{n-2}(X)$ is trivial
> (often denoted $\partial^2=0$ for simplicity).

*Proof.* Use $(\ast)$: the terms of $\partial_{n-1}\partial_nT$ cancel in pairs.
$\blacksquare$

> **Lemma 2.** $\partial_n\big(D_n(X)\big)\subseteq D_{n-1}(X)$ for all $n\ge1$.

*Proof.* Just use the definitions of $D_n$ and $\partial_n$. $\blacksquare$

Therefore $\partial_n$ induces a homomorphism $\partial_n:C_n(X)\to C_{n-1}(X)$,
$n\ge1$, i.e.

$$
u+D_n(X)\ \mapsto\ \partial_nu+D_{n-1}(X)
$$

is well defined. Set $\partial_n=0$ for $n\le0$. We get a sequence

$$
\cdots\xrightarrow{\partial_4}C_3(X)\xrightarrow{\partial_3}C_2(X)\xrightarrow{\partial_2}C_1(X)\xrightarrow{\partial_1}C_0(X)\xrightarrow{\partial_0}\underbrace{C_{-1}(X)}_{\{0\}}\xrightarrow{\partial_{-1}}\underbrace{C_{-2}(X)}_{\{0\}}\to\cdots
$$

> **Definition.**
>
> - $Z_n(X)=\ker\partial_n$, $n\in\mathbb{Z}$: the *$n$-cycles*;
> - $B_n(X)=\operatorname{im}\partial_{n+1}$, $n\in\mathbb{Z}$: the *$n$-boundaries*;
> - $H_n(X)=Z_n(X)/B_n(X)$ (by Lemma 1, $B_n(X)\subseteq Z_n(X)$): the
>   *$n$-dimensional singular homology group* (or $n$-th homology group).

**Trivial cases.** $D_0(X)=\{0\}$, so $C_0(X)=Q_0(X)$;
$C_n(X)=Z_n(X)=B_n(X)=H_n(X)=\{0\}$ for all $n<0$.

**Example 1.** $X=\emptyset$. The free abelian group generated by $\emptyset$ is
$\{0\}$, so $Q_n=D_n=C_n=Z_n=B_n=H_n=\{0\}$ for all $n\in\mathbb{Z}$.

**Example 2.** $X=\{pt\}$. There is exactly one singular $n$-cube for each $n\ge0$,
so $Q_n(pt)\cong\mathbb{Z}$ for $n\ge0$.

- $D_0(pt)=\{0\}\Rightarrow C_0(pt)\cong\mathbb{Z}\Rightarrow Z_0(pt)\cong\mathbb{Z}$.
- For $n\ge1$ the only cube is constant, hence degenerate: $D_n(pt)=Q_n(pt)\cong\mathbb{Z}$,
  so $C_n(pt)=\{0\}$ and $Z_n(pt)=B_n(pt)=\{0\}$ for $n\ge1$.

$$
\cdots\xrightarrow{\partial_3}\underbrace{C_2}_{\{0\}}\xrightarrow{\partial_2}\underbrace{C_1}_{\{0\}}\xrightarrow{\partial_1}\underbrace{C_0}_{\mathbb{Z}}\xrightarrow{\partial_0}\underbrace{C_{-1}}_{\{0\}}\to\cdots,
\qquad \partial_n=0 .
$$

Also $B_n(pt)=\operatorname{im}\partial_{n+1}=\{0\}$ for all $n\ge0$. Hence

$$
H_n(pt)=\{0\}\ (n\ne0),\qquad H_0(pt)\cong\mathbb{Z}.
$$

**Example 3.** $X$ a finite set with the discrete topology, $\operatorname{Card}(X)=k$.
Then $Q_n(X)\cong\mathbb{Z}^k$ for $n\ge0$ (a cube in a discrete space is constant),
and $C_0(X)\cong Z_0(X)\cong\mathbb{Z}^k$ since $D_0(X)=\{0\}$. Every $n$-cube is
degenerate for $n\ge1$, so $D_n(X)=Q_n(X)$ and $C_n(X)=\{0\}$ for $n\ge1$. Therefore

$$
H_0(X)\cong Z_0(X)\cong\mathbb{Z}^k,\qquad H_n(X)=Z_n(X)=B_n(X)=\{0\}\quad\forall n\ge1 .
$$

## 6.3 The homomorphism induced by a continuous map

Let $f:X\to Y$ be continuous. Construct $f_\#:Q_n(X)\to Q_n(Y)$: on cubes,

$$
f_\#(T)=f\circ T\in Q_n(Y),
$$

and $f_\#$ is the unique extension to a homomorphism:
$f_\#\big(\sum n_sT_s\big)=\sum n_sf_\#(T_s)=\sum n_s(f\circ T_s)$.

> **Lemma 3.** For $n\ge1$ the diagram
>
> $$
> \begin{array}{ccc}
> Q_n(X) & \xrightarrow{\ f_\#\ } & Q_n(Y)\\
> \big\downarrow{\scriptstyle\partial_n} & & \big\downarrow{\scriptstyle\partial_n}\\
> Q_{n-1}(X) & \xrightarrow{\ f_\#\ } & Q_{n-1}(Y)
> \end{array}
> $$
>
> is commutative.

*Proof.* $\partial_n(f_\#T)=\sum_i(-1)^i(A_if_\#T-B_if_\#T)$ and
$f_\#(\partial_nT)=\sum_i(-1)^i(f_\#A_iT-f_\#B_iT)$, and

$$
f_\#(A_iT)(x_1,\dots,x_{n-1})=(f\circ T)(x_1,\dots,x_{i-1},0,x_i,\dots,x_{n-1})=A_i(f_\#T)(x_1,\dots,x_{n-1}).
$$

Similarly $f_\#(B_iT)=B_i(f_\#T)$. $\blacksquare$

**Observe.**

1. $f_\#\big(D_n(X)\big)\subseteq D_n(Y)$. Hence $f_\#$ induces
   $f_\#:C_n(X)\to C_n(Y)$, $n\ge0$, and $\partial_nf_\#=f_\#\partial_n$ on
   $C_n(X)$ by Lemma 3.
2. $f_\#\big(Z_n(X)\big)\subseteq Z_n(Y)$, $n\ge1$: if $\partial_nu=0$ then
   $\partial_n(f_\#u)=f_\#(\partial_nu)=0$.
3. $f_\#\big(B_n(X)\big)\subseteq B_n(Y)$, $n\ge0$: if $u=\partial_{n+1}v$ with
   $v\in C_{n+1}(X)$, then $f_\#u=\partial_{n+1}(f_\#v)$ with $f_\#v\in C_{n+1}(Y)$.

Therefore $f_\#$ induces homomorphisms $f_*:H_n(X)\to H_n(Y)$ for all $n\ge1$.
Note $Z_0(X)=C_0(X)$, $Z_0(Y)=C_0(Y)$, so $f_\#(Z_0(X))\subseteq Z_0(Y)$ and $f_\#$
also induces $f_*:H_0(X)\to H_0(Y)$. Extending trivially to all $n\in\mathbb{Z}$,
$f_*:H_n(X)\to H_n(Y)$ is a homomorphism for every $n\in\mathbb{Z}$.

## 6.4 Homotopy invariance

> **Definition.** $X$ and $Y$ have the same *homotopy type* if there exist
> continuous $f:X\to Y$ and $g:Y\to X$ such that $f\circ g\simeq\iota_Y$ and
> $g\circ f\simeq\iota_X$. In this case $f,g$ are called *homotopy equivalences*.

Recall: $f_1,f_2:X\to Y$ are homotopic ($f_1\simeq f_2$) if there is a continuous
$H:I\times X\to Y$ with $H(0,x)=f_1(x)$, $H(1,x)=f_2(x)$.

> **Definition.** Let $A\subseteq X$, $B\subseteq Y$. A continuous map
> $f:(X,A)\to(Y,B)$ is a continuous map $f:X\to Y$ with $f(A)\subseteq B$. Two such
> maps $f,g:(X,A)\to(Y,B)$ are *homotopic* ($f\simeq g$) if there is a continuous
> map $H:(I\times X,\,I\times A)\to(Y,B)$ with $H(0,x)=f(x)$, $H(1,x)=g(x)$ (and
> $H(I\times A)\subseteq B$). $H$ is called a *homotopy* between $f$ and $g$.

> **Theorem 1.** $f\simeq g:X\to Y\ \Rightarrow\ f_*=g_*:H_n(X)\to H_n(Y)$ for all
> $n\in\mathbb{Z}$.

*Proof.* The case $n<0$ is trivial. Let $H:I\times X\to Y$ be a homotopy,
$H(0,x)=f(x)$, $H(1,x)=g(x)$. Construct
$\Phi_n:Q_n(X)\to Q_{n+1}(Y)$, $n\ge0$ (the *prism operator*):

$$
\Phi_n(T)(x_1,x_2,\dots,x_{n+1})=H\big(x_1,\,T(x_2,\dots,x_{n+1})\big),\qquad \Phi_{-1}=0 .
$$

Then $\Phi_n(T)(0,x_2,\dots)=f\circ T(x_2,\dots)$ and
$\Phi_n(T)(1,x_2,\dots)=g\circ T(x_2,\dots)$, i.e.

$$
A_1\circ\Phi_n=f_\#,\qquad B_1\circ\Phi_n=g_\# .
$$

For $2\le i\le n+1$,

$$
A_i\big(\Phi_n(T)\big)(x_1,\dots,x_n)=H\big(x_1,\,T(x_2,\dots,x_{i-1},0,x_i,\dots,x_n)\big)
=\Phi_{n-1}(A_{i-1}T)(x_1,\dots,x_n),
$$

so $A_i\circ\Phi_n=\Phi_{n-1}\circ A_{i-1}$, and similarly
$B_i\circ\Phi_n=\Phi_{n-1}\circ B_{i-1}$ for $2\le i\le n+1$.

**Relation between $\Phi_n$, $f$, $g$:**

$$
\begin{aligned}
\partial_{n+1}\Phi_n(T)&=\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}(-1)^i\big[A_i\Phi_n(T)-B_i\Phi_n(T)\big]\\
&=-A_1\Phi_n(T)+B_1\Phi_n(T)+\sum_{i=2}^{n+1}(-1)^i\big[\Phi_{n-1}A_{i-1}(T)-\Phi_{n-1}B_{i-1}(T)\big]\\
&=-f_\#(T)+g_\#(T)+\Phi_{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(-1)^{i+1}\big[A_i(T)-B_i(T)\big]\\
&=-f_\#(T)+g_\#(T)-\Phi_{n-1}\partial_n(T).
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, as homomorphisms $Q_n(X)\to Q_n(Y)$,

$$
-f_\#+g_\#=\partial_{n+1}\Phi_n+\Phi_{n-1}\partial_n,\qquad n\ge0 .
$$

If $T$ is degenerate, so are $\Phi_n(T)$, $f_\#(T)$ and $g_\#(T)$. Hence $\Phi_n$
induces $\varphi_n:C_n(X)\to C_{n+1}(Y)$,
$\varphi_n\big(u+D_n(X)\big):=\Phi_n(u)+D_{n+1}(Y)$ for $u\in Q_n(X)$, and

$$
-f_\#+g_\#=\partial_{n+1}\varphi_n+\varphi_{n-1}\partial_n,\qquad n\ge0,
$$

as homomorphisms $C_n(X)\to C_n(Y)$.

<video class="anim" src="assets/animations/PrismOperator.mp4" controls loop muted autoplay playsinline></video>

**Claim:** $f_*=g_*:H_n(X)\to H_n(Y)$. For $u\in H_n(X)$ write $u=u'+B_n(X)$ with
$u'\in Z_n(X)$. Then

$$
\begin{aligned}
-f_*(u)+g_*(u)&=-\big(f_\#(u')+B_n(Y)\big)+\big(g_\#(u')+B_n(Y)\big)
=(-f_\#+g_\#)(u')+B_n(Y)\\
&=\big(\partial_{n+1}\varphi_n+\varphi_{n-1}\partial_n\big)(u')+B_n(Y)
=\underbrace{\partial_{n+1}\big(\underbrace{\varphi_n(u')}_{\in C_{n+1}(Y)}\big)}_{\in B_n(Y)}+B_n(Y)=B_n(Y),
\end{aligned}
$$

using $\partial_nu'=0$. Hence $f_*=g_*$. $\blacksquare$

> **Lemma 4 (composition).** For continuous $f:X\to Y$, $g:Y\to Z$,
> $(g\circ f)_*=g_*\circ f_*:H_n(X)\to H_n(Z)$.

> **Theorem 2.** If $f:X\to Y$ is a homotopy equivalence, then
> $f_*:H_n(X)\to H_n(Y)$ is an isomorphism for all $n\ge0$.

*Proof.* Let $g:Y\to X$ with $f\circ g\simeq\iota_Y$, $g\circ f\simeq\iota_X$. In
$H_n(X)\xrightarrow{f_*}H_n(Y)\xrightarrow{g_*}H_n(X)$, by Lemma 4 and Theorem 1,

$$
f_*\circ g_*=(f\circ g)_*=(\iota_Y)_*=\iota_{H_n(Y)},\qquad
g_*\circ f_*=(g\circ f)_*=(\iota_X)_*=\iota_{H_n(X)} .
$$

So both $f_*$ and $g_*=f_*^{-1}$ are isomorphisms. $\blacksquare$

> **Corollary.** If $A\subseteq X$ is a deformation retract of $X$, then
> $H_n(A)\cong H_n(X)$ for all $n\in\mathbb{Z}$.

*Proof.* $n<0$ is obvious. For $n\ge0$, let $r:X\to A$ be a retraction with
$i\circ r\simeq\iota_X$, where $i:A\to X$ is the inclusion. Clearly
$r\circ i=\iota_A$. Thus $r$ is a homotopy equivalence, and by Theorem 2,
$H_n(A)\cong H_n(X)$ for $n\ge0$. $\blacksquare$

## 6.5 Relative homology

This motivates the study of *relative homology*. Consider a topological pair
$(X,A)$ (i.e. $X$ a space, $A\subseteq X$), and assign to it groups $H_n(X,A)$.

The $n$-chains of $(X,A)$:

$$
C_n(X,A)=\text{free abelian group on (non-degenerate) singular $n$-cubes in $X$ whose image}\not\subseteq A
\;=\;C_n(X)/C_n(A).
$$

Since $\partial_n\big(C_n(A)\big)\subseteq C_{n-1}(A)$, the boundary
$C_n(X)\xrightarrow{\partial_n}C_{n-1}(X)$ induces

$$
\cdots\xrightarrow{\partial_{n+1}}C_n(X,A)\xrightarrow{\partial_n}C_{n-1}(X,A)\xrightarrow{\partial_{n-1}}\cdots,
\qquad\partial^2=0 .
$$

> **Definition.**
> $Z_n(X,A)=\ker\partial_n$ ($n$-cycles), $B_n(X,A)=\operatorname{im}\partial_{n+1}$
> ($n$-boundaries), and
> $H_n(X,A)=\ker\partial_n/\operatorname{im}\partial_{n+1}$, the *$n$-th relative
> homology group*.

Let $i_\#:C_n(A)\to C_n(X)$ be induced by the inclusion and
$j_\#:C_n(X)\to C_n(X,A)$ the projection. For each $n$ the rows are exact and the
squares commute:

$$
\begin{array}{ccccccccc}
0 & \to & C_n(A) & \xrightarrow{\ i_\#\ } & C_n(X) & \xrightarrow{\ j_\#\ } & C_n(X,A) & \to & 0\\
 & & \big\downarrow{\scriptstyle\partial_n} & & \big\downarrow{\scriptstyle\partial_n} & & \big\downarrow{\scriptstyle\partial_n} & & \\
0 & \to & C_{n-1}(A) & \xrightarrow{\ i_\#\ } & C_{n-1}(X) & \xrightarrow{\ j_\#\ } & C_{n-1}(X,A) & \to & 0
\end{array}
$$

We will end up with a long sequence of groups

$$
\cdots\to H_n(A)\xrightarrow{\ i_*\ }H_n(X)\xrightarrow{\ j_*\ }H_n(X,A)
\xrightarrow{\ \partial_*\ }H_{n-1}(A)\xrightarrow{\ i_*\ }H_{n-1}(X)\xrightarrow{\ j_*\ }H_{n-1}(X,A)
\xrightarrow{\ \partial_*\ }\cdots
$$

It is called the **homology sequence of the pair $(X,A)$**. The maps $i_*,j_*$ are
induced by $i_\#,j_\#$; the *connecting homomorphism* $\partial_*$ is defined below.

## 6.6 The connecting homomorphism $\partial_*$

Let $u\in H_n(X,A)=Z_n(X,A)/B_n(X,A)$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\Rightarrow\ u=u'+B_n(X,A),\qquad u'\in Z_n(X,A)\subseteq C_n(X,A)=C_n(X)/C_n(A),\\
&\Rightarrow\ u'=u''+C_n(A),\qquad u''\in C_n(X),\ \text{ i.e. } j_\#(u'')=u',\\
&B_{n-1}(X,A)\ni 0=\partial_nu'=\partial_nu''+C_{n-1}(A)\\
&\Rightarrow\ \partial_nu''\in C_{n-1}(A),\qquad \partial_nu''\in Z_{n-1}(A)\quad(\text{since }\partial_{n-1}\partial_nu''=0).
\end{aligned}
$$

Define

$$
\partial_*u:=\partial_nu''+B_{n-1}(A)\ \in\ H_{n-1}(A).
$$

**Exercise.** The definition of $\partial_*u$ is independent of the choices of $u'$ and $u''$.

In the diagram of §6.5: start at $u'$ in the top right, lift along $j_\#$ to $u''$, go down
by $\partial_n$, and the result comes back along $i_\#$ from $C_{n-1}(A)$.

## 6.7 Exact sequences

> **Definition.** A sequence of groups $G_i$ and homomorphisms $f_i$
>
> $$
> \cdots\to G_1\xrightarrow{f_1}G_2\xrightarrow{f_2}G_3\xrightarrow{f_3}G_4\xrightarrow{f_4}\cdots
> $$
>
> is *exact* if $\operatorname{im}f_i=\ker f_{i+1}$ for all $i$.

A *short exact sequence* is an exact sequence of the form

$$
0\xrightarrow{\ a\ }G_1\xrightarrow{f_1}G_2\xrightarrow{f_2}G_3\xrightarrow{\ b\ }0 .
$$

A *long exact sequence* is an exact sequence of the form

$$
\cdots\xrightarrow{f_{-4}}G_{-3}\xrightarrow{f_{-3}}G_{-2}\xrightarrow{f_{-2}}G_{-1}\xrightarrow{f_{-1}}G_0\xrightarrow{f_0}G_1\xrightarrow{f_1}G_2\to\cdots
$$

When only finitely many $G_i$ are nontrivial, write it as

$$
0\to G_n\xrightarrow{f_n}G_{n+1}\xrightarrow{f_{n+1}}G_{n+2}\to\cdots\xrightarrow{f_{m-1}}G_m\to0 .
$$

> **Proposition** (from algebra, e.g. Lang, *Algebra*, p. 40). Let $A\xrightarrow{f}A'$ be
> a surjective homomorphism of abelian groups, and $A'$ free. Then
> $A\cong A'\oplus\ker f$.

> **Theorem.** Let $0\to G_1\xrightarrow{f}G_2\xrightarrow{g}G_3\to0$ be a short exact
> sequence of abelian groups. Suppose $G_3$ is free. Then $G_2\cong G_1\oplus G_3$.
>
> In this case we say the short exact sequence *splits*.

*Proof.* Clearly $f$ is 1–1 and $g$ is onto. By the proposition above,

$$
G_2\cong G_3\oplus\ker g=G_3\oplus\operatorname{im}f\cong G_3\oplus G_1\cong G_1\oplus G_3 .
\qquad\blacksquare
$$

## 6.8 Reduced homology

The *augmentation* $\varepsilon:C_0(X)\to\mathbb{Z}$ is defined by $\varepsilon(T)=1$ for
every 0-cube $T$, extended linearly:

$$
\varepsilon\Big(\sum_in_iT_i\Big)=\sum_in_i,\qquad
\cdots\to C_1(X)\xrightarrow{\partial_1}C_0(X)\xrightarrow{\ \varepsilon\ }\mathbb{Z}.
$$

**Note.** $\varepsilon\circ\partial_1=0$: for a path $T$ from $p$ to $q$,
$\varepsilon\partial_1(T)=\varepsilon(q-p)=1-1=0$.

Replacing $\partial_0$ by $\varepsilon$, we get another sequence of homology groups; the only
difference is in degree $0$:

$$
\tilde H_0(X):=\ker\varepsilon/\operatorname{im}\partial_1\ \subseteq\ C_0(X)/\operatorname{im}\partial_1=H_0(X),
\qquad
\tilde Z_0(X):=\ker\varepsilon,\quad \tilde B_0(X):=\operatorname{im}\partial_1,\quad
\tilde H_0(X)=\tilde Z_0/\tilde B_0 .
$$

Since $\varepsilon(B_0(X))=0$, $\varepsilon$ induces $\varepsilon_*:H_0(X)\to\mathbb{Z}$, and we
get

$$
0\to\tilde H_0(X)\xrightarrow{\ \xi\ }H_0(X)\xrightarrow{\ \varepsilon_*\ }\mathbb{Z}\to0,
\qquad \xi=\text{inclusion}.
$$

> **Theorem.** For $X\neq\emptyset$ the sequence above is exact and splits.

*Proof.* Clearly $\xi$ is 1–1, and $\varepsilon_*$ is onto (a single 0-cube maps to $1$).
We need $\operatorname{im}\xi=\ker\varepsilon_*$:

$$
\varepsilon_*\big(u+B_0(X)\big)=0\iff\varepsilon(u)=0\iff u\in\ker\varepsilon=\tilde Z_0
\iff u+B_0=u+\tilde B_0\in\tilde H_0(X)=\operatorname{im}\xi .
$$

$\mathbb{Z}$ is free, so the sequence splits. $\blacksquare$

> **Corollary.** $H_0(X)\cong\tilde H_0(X)\oplus\mathbb{Z}$.

Let $\tilde H_n(X)=H_n(X)$ for $n\neq0$. $\{\tilde H_n(X)\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}$ is the sequence of
**reduced homology groups**. (Massey §VII.2.3, p. 161.)

## 6.9 Back to relative homology

> **Theorem.** If $f,g:(X,A)\to(Y,B)$ are maps of topological pairs and $f\simeq g$, then
> $f_*=g_*:H_n(X,A)\to H_n(Y,B)$ for all $n$.

*Proof.* Similar to the case $H_n(X)\to H_n(Y)$ (§6.4). $\blacksquare$

> **Theorem.** If $f:(X,A)\to(Y,B)$ is a homotopy equivalence, then
> $f_*:H_n(X,A)\to H_n(Y,B)$ is an isomorphism for all $n\in\mathbb{Z}$.

> **Five Lemma** (Steenrod). Suppose the diagram
>
> $$
> \begin{array}{ccccccccc}
> A_1 & \xrightarrow{i_1} & A_2 & \xrightarrow{i_2} & A_3 & \xrightarrow{i_3} & A_4 & \xrightarrow{i_4} & A_5\\
> \big\downarrow{\scriptstyle f_1} & & \big\downarrow{\scriptstyle f_2} & & \big\downarrow{\scriptstyle f_3} & & \big\downarrow{\scriptstyle f_4} & & \big\downarrow{\scriptstyle f_5}\\
> B_1 & \xrightarrow{j_1} & B_2 & \xrightarrow{j_2} & B_3 & \xrightarrow{j_3} & B_4 & \xrightarrow{j_4} & B_5
> \end{array}
> $$
>
> commutes, where the $A_i,B_i$ are groups, the $f_i,i_i,j_i$ are homomorphisms, and each row
> is exact. Then
>
> 1. $f_2,f_4$ 1–1 and $f_1$ onto $\Rightarrow$ $f_3$ is 1–1;
> 2. $f_2,f_4$ onto and $f_5$ 1–1 $\Rightarrow$ $f_3$ is onto.
>
> In particular, if $f_1,f_2,f_4,f_5$ are isomorphisms, so is $f_3$.

The groups need not be abelian; we write them multiplicatively, with identity $1$.

*Proof of (1).* Let $x\in\ker f_3$, i.e. $f_3(x)=1$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\Rightarrow\ 1=j_3(f_3(x))=f_4(i_3(x))\ \Rightarrow\ i_3(x)=1 &&(f_4\ \text{1–1})\\
&\Rightarrow\ x\in\ker i_3=\operatorname{im}i_2\ \Rightarrow\ \exists\,y\in A_2:\ i_2(y)=x
 \ \Rightarrow\ j_2(f_2(y))=f_3(i_2(y))=1\\
&\Rightarrow\ f_2(y)\in\ker j_2=\operatorname{im}j_1\ \Rightarrow\ \exists\,z\in B_1:\ j_1(z)=f_2(y)\\
&f_1\ \text{onto}\ \Rightarrow\ \exists\,w\in A_1:\ f_1(w)=z\\
&f_2(i_1(w))=j_1(f_1(w))=j_1(z)=f_2(y)\ \Rightarrow\ y=i_1(w) &&(f_2\ \text{1–1})\\
&\Rightarrow\ y\in\operatorname{im}i_1=\ker i_2\ \Rightarrow\ 1=i_2(y)=x .
\end{aligned}
$$

$\therefore f_3$ is 1–1.

*Proof of (2).* Let $x\in B_3$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
&f_4\ \text{onto}\ \Rightarrow\ \exists\,y\in A_4:\ f_4(y)=j_3(x)\\
&\Rightarrow\ f_5(i_4(y))=j_4(f_4(y))=j_4(j_3(x))=1\ \Rightarrow\ i_4(y)=1 &&(f_5\ \text{1–1})\\
&\Rightarrow\ y\in\ker i_4=\operatorname{im}i_3\ \Rightarrow\ \exists\,z\in A_3:\ i_3(z)=y\\
&j_3\big(f_3(z)^{-1}x\big)=j_3(f_3(z))^{-1}j_3(x)=f_4(i_3(z))^{-1}f_4(y)=f_4(y)^{-1}f_4(y)=1\\
&\Rightarrow\ f_3(z)^{-1}x\in\ker j_3=\operatorname{im}j_2\ \Rightarrow\ \exists\,w\in B_2:\ j_2(w)=f_3(z)^{-1}x\\
&\Rightarrow\ \exists\,u\in A_2:\ f_2(u)=w &&(f_2\ \text{onto})\\
&\Rightarrow\ f_3\big(z\cdot i_2(u)\big)=f_3(z)\,f_3(i_2(u))=f_3(z)\,j_2(f_2(u))=f_3(z)\,j_2(w)=f_3(z)f_3(z)^{-1}x=x .
\end{aligned}
$$

$\therefore f_3$ is onto. $\blacksquare$
